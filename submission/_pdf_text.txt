Abstract
We compare Armijo gradient descent (GD), safeguarded nonlinear conjugate gra-
dient (CG), and BFGS on Rosenbrock and Rastrigin through 90 controlled CPU
solves. Dimensions 2, 10, and 20 each use ﬁve paired initial points and ﬁxed stop-
ping criteria. In the recorded Windows reference run, 73/90 runs meet the gradient
tolerance and 26/90 meet the global-objective tolerance: 47 meet only the gradient
criterion, while 17 meet neither. On Rosenbrock, CG and BFGS attain the global-
objective target in 14/15 and 12/15 cases; GD reaches the iteration cap in all 15
cases. On Rastrigin, 43/45 runs meet the gradient tolerance, but none meets the
global-objective target. Endpoint diagnostics and comparisons conditional on CG
and BFGS both meeting the global-objective target separate local convergence,
evaluation cost, and ﬁnal objective quality. Complete traces support this empirical
distinction.
1 Introduction and scope
Smooth unconstrained optimization is a useful setting for studying how descent directions, line
searches, curvature information, and stopping rules interact. It also exposes a practical ambiguity:
software may report successful termination at a point with a small gradient even when its objective
remains far from the global minimum. Comparing iteration counts alone can conceal both this
difference in solution quality and the work spent rejecting trial steps.
This individual project belongs to Unconstrained Optimization. The variables range over all of
real Euclidean space, and the objectives are nonconvex. The group project selected the Convex
Optimization direction; the present investigation instead studies local algorithms outside convex-
ity. Rosenbrock supplies a curved valley and coupled coordinates, while Rastrigin supplies many
attraction basins and separable oscillations. Both have known global objective value zero, making
stationarity and global-objective quality independently measurable [ 7, 8].
Three questions guide the comparison. First, which methods reach the common gradient tolerance
within a ﬁxed accepted-update budget? Second, what function and gradient evaluation costs accom-
pany their outcomes across dimensions and paired starts? Third, when termination is unsuccessful
or non-global, what do the saved traces and endpoint curvature actually support? The design ﬁxes
the implementations and settings before comparing all 90 outcomes, including failures.

2 Literature review and connection to the course
Classical foundation. Nocedal and Wright [ 1] organize smooth unconstrained methods around a
search direction and an inexact line search. Their treatment connects ﬁrst-order stationarity, descent,
quasi-Newton curvature, and local convergence. These concepts provide the classroom framework
here: an update can decrease the objective while remaining slow, and a necessary ﬁrst-order condi-
tion becomes sufﬁcient for global optimality only under additional structure such as convexity. Our
nonconvex functions deliberately remove that sufﬁciency.
Step selection and memory. Armijo’s early work [ 2] established a sufﬁcient-decrease approach to
choosing step lengths. GD uses the current negative gradient, making its behavior easy to audit
but sensitive to geometry. Nonlinear CG carries information from the preceding search direction.
Gilbert and Nocedal [ 3] analyze globally convergent CG variants with appropriate safeguards and
line searches on smooth nonconvex problems. Such results depend on the precise variant and as-
sumptions; they do not imply that an arbitrary Polak–Ribiere implementation or a positive β alone
ensures descent. The PR+ implementation used here therefore warrants source-level identiﬁcation.
Quasi-Newton methods. Fletcher’s variable-metric development [ 4] is part of the foundation of
BFGS. BFGS learns curvature from successive displacements and gradient differences instead of
forming an analytic Hessian. This can improve local progress, at the cost of matrix storage and
updates. Positive-deﬁnite inverse-Hessian approximations yield descent directions in exact arith-
metic, but neither this property nor a Wolfe line search certiﬁes a global minimizer on a nonconvex
objective. Implementation details and ﬁnite precision remain relevant when a solver stops near its
tolerance.
Recent theory. Jin, Jiang, and Mokhtari [ 5] obtain explicit non-asymptotic rates for BFGS with
Armijo–Wolfe line search under strong convexity and smoothness; additional regularity supports
stronger rate statements. Their analysis clariﬁes the gap between qualitative convergence and ﬁnite-
iteration performance. Strong convexity is absent globally in our tests, so these rates provide context
rather than a theorem for the recorded runs. Mannel [ 6] studies a modiﬁed, globalized L-BFGS and
Barzilai–Borwein framework for nonconvex unconstrained optimization, using cautious updates and
conditions supporting stationarity of cluster points. This is a different algorithm from ordinary
full-memory SciPy BFGS. Its relevance is that nonconvex guarantees require carefully designed
safeguards, rather than a change of terminology.

Known optima and nonconvexity. Both models are sums of nonnegative terms. Rosenbrock reaches
zero only at the all-ones vector for d ≥ 2; Rastrigin reaches zero only at the zero vector. In two-
dimensional Rosenbrock, the Hessian at (0,1) is diag(-398,200), so the function is not convex. This
point is not a saddle point: its gradient is (-2,200), which is nonzero. For Rastrigin the Hessian
is diagonal with entries 2 + 40 π2 cos(2πxi); a coordinate equal to 1/2 yields negative curvature.
Negative curvature at an arbitrary point establishes nonconvexity, not stationarity.
The coupled Rosenbrock valley makes curvature vary strongly with direction. Rastrigin is separable,
yet its oscillatory terms produce many local basins; coordinatewise independence therefore does not
make a local descent method a global solver. Both functions are smooth and coercive, so bounded
objective sublevels are compact. Their gradients have suitable local Lipschitz bounds on bounded
regions, but Rosenbrock’s gradient is not globally Lipschitz over all of Rd. At the two-dimensional
Rosenbrock global minimizer, the Hessian has eigenvalues approximately 0.39936 and 1001.60064,
giving condition number about 2508. This local ill-conditioning is consistent with slow GD progress;
it is not a rate estimate for the full nonlinear trajectory. These distinctions matter when stating
descent guarantees.

Evaluation accounting. A wrapper counts every actual raw objective and gradient call made by
a solver, including rejected line-search trials and trials in a failed terminal search. Calls avoided
by SciPy’s cache are not invented. The wrapper counts must equal SciPy’s returned nfev/njev.
Callbacks only copy accepted coordinates and snapshot counters; they do not reevaluate f or g. Each
trace contains an initial event with counters zero, one event per accepted update, and a terminal
event. Initial plot diagnostics and every recorded objective/gradient norm are ﬁlled ofﬂine from
raw functions, outside the counters and timer. Thus the initial point can appear at nfev=0 without
making the solver’s own initial evaluation free.
The study records 80,605 events: 80,425 accepted updates and 180 initial/terminal events. Solver
time covers evaluations, updates, and event capture, excluding imports, ofﬂine diagnostics, ﬁle I/O,
and plotting. The sum of measured solver times is 26.915 s, rather than total pipeline wall time.
Timing is secondary because these small problems are sensitive to Python, callback, and scheduling
overhead. Figures stop at each actual endpoint; no curve is extended after termination, and log-
display ﬂoors never change raw values.

9 Discussion, limitations, and reproducibility
Interpretation of the three questions. First, in the recorded Windows reference run, 73/90 runs
meet the common gradient criterion, with failures concentrated in Rosenbrock GD and two 20D
Rastrigin BFGS runs. Second, function and gradient counts reveal costs hidden by accepted-update
counts; the terminal 58/46 extra calls are a concrete example. The median ratios favor BFGS in
function evaluations on the selected Rosenbrock pairs where both methods meet G, whereas CG
uses fewer function evaluations than GD to its observed Rastrigin stationary endpoints. Third, ﬁnal
f and endpoint diagnostics show that meeting the gradient criterion does not imply meeting the
global-objective target: 47 runs meet S while missing G. These observations connect descent and
curvature concepts from the course to actual solver behavior.
Limits of the evidence. The study includes only two synthetic functions, three dimensions up to
20, and ﬁve ﬁxed starts per setting. Its observed fractions are not estimated population success
probabilities. Dimensions and changing random starts vary together, so trends across dimensions
are descriptive. The experiment neither establishes asymptotic scaling nor measures the practical
memory limit of BFGS. A single implementation and one set of line-search constants also cannot
isolate the effects of direction, step selection, initialization, tolerance, and numerical safeguards.
Millisecond timings do not justify a universal fastest-method claim, and conditioning cost ratios on
both methods attaining G creates an explicitly selected subset.
Platform scope. The tables describe the recorded Windows run. Pinning NumPy and SciPy versions
does not guarantee identical ﬂoating-point paths across operating systems, Python builds, proces-
sors, or linked numerical libraries. Small arithmetic differences can change line-search decisions,
evaluation counts, and classiﬁcations near the strict 10−6 threshold. A different execution path does
not, by itself, invalidate the saved Windows records. Such differences require trace-level diagnosis;
this study does not isolate a platform-speciﬁc cause.
Useful follow-up work would vary GD’s initial trial-step policy under the same starts, sweep the
stopping tolerance and iteration budget, and compare damped or limited-memory quasi-Newton
variants using a larger test collection. Multi-start global search would address basin selection but
would answer a different question from this ﬁxed local-method comparison. A real-data smooth ob-
jective would improve external relevance while usually removing access to a known global optimum.
These are future experiments, not results claimed here.
9.1 Reproducibility and access
The archive’s README gives ﬁle access, exact commands, and audit scopes. Historical Windows-
to-Windows comparison records are labelled separately from the archived unit tests and read-only
data/source checks. These records establish neither a new formal experiment nor cross-platform
numerical identity.
10 Conclusion
Under the ﬁxed protocol, convergence to a small-gradient point did not imply a small global-
objective gap. Rosenbrock favored CG and BFGS over this GD conﬁguration within the iteration
budget, but both sometimes reached non-global near-stationary points. On Rastrigin, all global-
objective targets were missed despite 43/45 runs meeting the gradient criterion; GD occasionally
obtained better objective values while using more function evaluations. The two BFGS precision-
loss terminations were retained, including one just above tolerance. These results support separate
reporting of stationarity, objective quality, costs, and failure modes.
