 Stationarity, Evaluation Cost, and Solution Quality
 in Nonconvex Unconstrained Optimization
 A controlled CPU comparison of GD, PR+ conjugate gradient, and BFGS
 Yangxiao Cai | Student ID: 26023041r
COMP6704 Individual Assignment, Fall 2026
Primary direction: Unconstrained Optimization
Abstract
We compare Armijo gradient descent (GD), safeguarded nonlinear conjugate gradient (CG), and BFGS on Rosenbrock
and Rastrigin through 90 controlled CPU solves. Dimensions 2, 10, and 20 each use five paired initial points and fixed
stopping criteria. Analytic gradients, cumulative evaluation counts, accepted iterates, and raw termination statuses are
audited. Overall, 73/90 runs meet the gradient tolerance and 26/90 meet the known global-objective tolerance: 47 meet
only the gradient criterion, while 17 meet neither. On Rosenbrock, CG and BFGS attain the global target in 14/15 and
12/15 cases; GD reaches the iteration cap in all 15 cases. On Rastrigin, 43/45 runs meet the gradient tolerance, but none
meets the global target. Endpoint diagnostics and comparisons conditional on matched global-target outcomes separate
local convergence, evaluation cost, and final objective quality. This study contributes reproducible empirical evidence
for these distinctions, without proposing a new algorithm or a universal optimizer ranking. The PDF embeds the
complete reproducibility archive as a file attachment.
1. Introduction and scope
Smooth unconstrained optimization is a useful setting for studying how descent directions, line searches, curvature
information, and stopping rules interact. It also exposes a practical ambiguity: software may report successful
termination at a point with a small gradient even when its objective remains far from the global minimum. Comparing
iteration counts alone can conceal both this difference in solution quality and the work spent rejecting trial steps.
This individual project belongs to Unconstrained Optimization. The variables range over all of real Euclidean space,
and the objectives are nonconvex. The group project selected the Convex Optimization direction; the present
investigation instead studies local algorithms outside convexity. Rosenbrock supplies a curved valley and coupled
coordinates, while Rastrigin supplies many attraction basins and separable oscillations. Both have known global
objective value zero, making stationarity and global-objective quality independently measurable [1, 8].
Three questions guide the comparison. First, which methods reach the common gradient tolerance within a fixed
accepted-update budget? Second, what function and gradient evaluation costs accompany their outcomes across
dimensions and paired starts? Third, when termination is unsuccessful or non-global, what do the saved traces and
endpoint curvature actually support? The design fixes the implementations and settings before comparing all 90
outcomes; it retains failures and publishes the initial points, logs, analysis, and document sources.

2. Literature review and connection to the course
Classical foundation. Nocedal and Wright [1] organize smooth unconstrained methods around a search direction and an
inexact line search. Their treatment connects first-order stationarity, descent, quasi-Newton curvature, and local
convergence. These concepts provide the classroom framework here: an update can decrease the objective while
remaining slow, and a necessary first-order condition becomes sufficient for global optimality only under additional
structure such as convexity. Our nonconvex functions deliberately remove that sufficiency.
Step selection and memory. Armijo's early work [2] established a sufficient-decrease approach to choosing step lengths.
GD uses the current negative gradient, making its behavior easy to audit but sensitive to geometry. Nonlinear CG carries
information from the preceding search direction. Gilbert and Nocedal [3] analyze globally convergent CG variants with
appropriate safeguards and line searches on smooth nonconvex problems. Such results depend on the precise variant and
assumptions; they do not imply that an arbitrary Polak–Ribiere implementation or a positive beta alone ensures descent.
The PR+ implementation used here therefore warrants source-level identification.
Quasi-Newton methods. Fletcher's variable-metric development [4] is part of the foundation of BFGS. BFGS learns
curvature from successive displacements and gradient differences instead of forming an analytic Hessian. This can
improve local progress, at the cost of matrix storage and updates. Positive-definite inverse-Hessian approximations
yield descent directions in exact arithmetic, but neither this property nor a Wolfe line search certifies a global minimizer
on a nonconvex objective. Implementation details and finite precision remain relevant when a solver stops near its
tolerance.
Recent theory. Jin, Jiang, and Mokhtari [5] obtain explicit non-asymptotic rates for BFGS with Armijo–Wolfe line
search under strong convexity and smoothness; additional regularity supports stronger rate statements. Their analysis
clarifies the gap between qualitative convergence and finite-iteration performance. Strong convexity is absent globally
in our tests, so these rates provide context rather than a theorem for the recorded runs. Mannel [6] studies a modified,
globalized L-BFGS and Barzilai–Borwein framework for nonconvex unconstrained optimization, using cautious
updates and conditions supporting stationarity of cluster points. This is a different algorithm from ordinary full-memory
SciPy BFGS. Its relevance is that nonconvex guarantees require carefully designed safeguards, rather than a change of
terminology.

For Rosenbrock, each term indexed by i contributes -400 x_i(x_{i+1}-x_i^2) + 2(x_i-1) to derivative component i and
200(x_{i+1}-x_i^2) to component i+1. The implementation accumulates all adjacent contributions. Before solving,
central differences at 12 nonstationary test points check both gradients, using h_i = 10^-6 max(1, |x_i|) and
componentwise absolute and relative tolerances 10^-5. All 12 checks pass; those checks are outside solver evaluation
counts.
Known optima and nonconvexity. Both models are sums of nonnegative terms. Rosenbrock reaches zero only at the
all-ones vector for d >= 2; Rastrigin reaches zero only at the zero vector. In two-dimensional Rosenbrock, the Hessian at
(0,1) is diag(-398,200), so the function is not convex. This point is not a saddle point: its gradient is (-2,200), which is
nonzero. For Rastrigin the Hessian is diagonal with entries 2 + 40 pi^2 cos(2 pi x_i); a coordinate equal to 1/2 yields
negative curvature. Negative curvature at an arbitrary point establishes nonconvexity, not stationarity.
The coupled Rosenbrock valley makes curvature vary strongly with direction. Rastrigin is separable, yet its oscillatory
terms produce many local basins; coordinatewise independence therefore does not make a local descent method a global
solver. Both functions are smooth and coercive, so bounded objective sublevels are compact. Their gradients have
suitable local Lipschitz bounds on bounded regions, but Rosenbrock's gradient is not globally Lipschitz over all of R^d.
These distinctions matter when stating descent guarantees.
4. Algorithms and implementation
4.1 Gradient descent with Armijo backtracking
(4)
GD is implemented directly. Every iteration starts again at alpha_0 = 1, halves the trial step with tau = 0.5, and accepts
the first point satisfying (4), with c1 = 10^-4. At most 50 reductions follow the initial trial, and the minimum step is
10^-12. A rejected trial never updates x. Nonfinite values, an unchanged floating-point point, and an exhausted line
search receive explicit failure statuses. The accepted-update limit is 5000; all function calls at rejected trial points still
count.

The intervals above generate initial points only; they do not constrain subsequent iterates. Each function-dimension pair
has its own random stream, and each of its five saved starts is shared across methods. This pairing controls starting
difficulty without asserting that five points represent all possible initializations. The two tolerances define different
questions. Native solver_success and S happen to agree for these 90 runs, but remain distinct fields; G is a
known-objective target rather than a mathematical certificate of exact optimality.
Evaluation accounting. A wrapper counts every actual raw objective and gradient call made by a solver, including
rejected line-search trials and trials in a failed terminal search. Calls avoided by SciPy's cache are not invented. The
wrapper counts must equal SciPy's returned nfev/njev. Callbacks only copy accepted coordinates and snapshot counters;
they do not reevaluate f or g. Each trace contains an initial event with counters zero, one event per accepted update, and
a terminal event. Initial plot diagnostics and every recorded objective/gradient norm are filled offline from raw
functions, outside the counters and timer. Thus the initial point can appear at nfev=0 without making the solver's own
initial evaluation free.
The study records 80,605 events: 80,425 accepted updates and 180 initial/terminal events. Solver time covers
evaluations, updates, and event capture, excluding imports, offline diagnostics, file I/O, and plotting. The sum of
measured solver times is 26.915 s, rather than total pipeline wall time. Timing is secondary because these small
problems are sensitive to Python, callback, and scheduling overhead. Figures stop at each actual endpoint; no curve is
extended after termination, and log-display floors never change raw values.

9. Discussion, limitations, and reproducibility
Interpretation of the three questions. First, a common gradient tolerance yields a transparent local convergence
comparison: 73/90 runs pass, with failures concentrated in Rosenbrock GD and two 20D Rastrigin BFGS runs. Second,
function and gradient counts reveal costs hidden by accepted-update counts; the terminal 58/46 extra calls are a concrete
example. BFGS uses fewer function calls on Rosenbrock's matched-global subset, whereas CG uses fewer calls than
GD to its observed Rastrigin stationary endpoints. Third, final f and endpoint diagnostics show that local success and
global quality are separate: 47 runs meet S while missing G. These observations connect descent and curvature concepts
from the course to actual solver behavior.
Limits of the evidence. The study includes only two synthetic functions, three dimensions up to 20, and five fixed starts
per setting. Its observed fractions are not estimated population success probabilities. Dimensions and changing random
starts vary together, so trends across dimensions are descriptive. The experiment neither establishes asymptotic scaling
nor measures the practical memory limit of BFGS. A single implementation and one set of line-search constants also
cannot isolate the effects of direction, step selection, initialization, tolerance, and numerical safeguards. Millisecond
timings do not justify a universal fastest-method claim, and conditioning cost ratios on both methods attaining G creates
an explicitly selected subset.
Useful follow-up work would vary GD's initial trial-step policy under the same starts, sweep the stopping tolerance and
iteration budget, and compare damped or limited-memory quasi-Newton variants using a larger test collection.
Multi-start global search would address basin selection but would answer a different question from this fixed
local-method comparison. A real-data smooth objective would improve external relevance while usually removing
access to a known global optimum. These are future experiments, not results claimed here.

elapsed times and timestamps are deliberately excluded from equality checks.
10. Conclusion
Under the fixed protocol, convergence to a small-gradient point did not imply a small global-objective gap. Rosenbrock
favored CG and BFGS over this GD configuration within the iteration budget, but both sometimes reached non-global
near-stationary points. On Rastrigin, all global targets were missed despite 43/45 stationarity successes; GD
occasionally obtained better objective values while using more evaluations. The two BFGS precision-loss terminations
were retained, including one just above tolerance. These results support separate reporting of stationarity, objective
quality, costs, and failure modes. Conclusions remain limited to the tested functions, starts, dimensions, and
implementations.
